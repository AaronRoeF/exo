# lantern — Teach Concepts That Resist the Reader

**WHY:** Spiritual and psychological concepts aren't hard because they're complicated — they're hard because the reader's ego is both the student and the subject matter. Direct statement triggers defense ("you judge people to feel superior" — door slams). Every great teacher converged on indirect transmission: the prophet Nathan's parable made King David convict himself before "You are the man" (2 Samuel 12). LANTERN is that technology with the parts labeled.

**WHO:** Calibrate to the actual reader (skillful means: the Buddha gave the musician a lute-string simile, not a farming one). For an executive: boardrooms, negotiations, hiring, parenting. For a **universal** audience, draw Anchors from near-universal dailies — waking and sleep, phones, kitchens, traffic, waiting rooms, family meals — and keep the same 72-hour test; a universal Turn still names a specific, recent, answerable moment ("in the last two weeks…"), just from common life rather than the reader's profession.

**HOW:** Seven moves, L→A→N→T→E→R→N, always in order. The order does the work: crack open (Line), embody (Anchor), enter sideways (Narrative), spring the mirror (Turn), convert to first-person evidence (Enact), show why to persist (Ripples), name the counterfeit (Nemesis).

---

## The seven moves

| | Move | The move | Quality test |
|---|------|----------|--------------|
| **L** | Line | One compressed, slightly paradoxical aphorism | Quotable a week later; itches, doesn't soothe; ≤ ~12 words |
| **A** | Anchor | Physical daily-life image that triggers recall | Reader physically encounters it within ~72 hours |
| **N** | Narrative | Parable **with a reversal** | Ending inverts expectation or indicts the "obvious" hero |
| **T** | Turn | Mirror question, second person, specific | Slightly uncomfortable aloud; answerable from the last two weeks |
| **E** | Enact | One experiment, <5 min, within 24h, no equipment | Observable result; experiment, not habit commitment |
| **R** | Ripples | 2nd-order (weeks, around you) + 3rd-order (years, systems) | Mechanisms, not vibes |
| **N** | Nemesis | The ego's counterfeit of this exact virtue | Recognizable failure mode + a discriminating test |

## How to run it

1. **Research before rendering.** If the concept comes from a corpus (a book, a tradition), get verified primary sources first. Never launder apocrypha into a Narrative — cite classical stories, attribute true ones, and *label constructed parables as constructed*. Claims that can't be sourced carry `[INFERENCE]`.
2. **L — Line.** If the tradition already minted the coin ("Sell your cleverness and buy bewilderment" — Rumi, Masnavi IV:1407), spend it with citation. Excerpting a clause of a verified line, with citation, still counts as spending the coin; altering its words does not. The ≤ ~12-word bar applies to Lines you write yourself. Write your own only when the original needs translation to land.
3. **A — Anchor.** Pick the object/scene the *actual reader* will hit this week: the pros-and-cons list after the gut already decided, the loaded rebuttal while the other person is still talking, the child's misspelled card. Abstract "images" (oceans of consciousness) fail the 72-hour test.
4. **N — Narrative.** Reversal is mandatory — a story that merely illustrates is decoration; the twist is the argument. Sourcing hierarchy: classical (cited) → true story (attributed) → constructed (labeled). A classical story known from general knowledge but unverified in your working corpus may be used with an explicit "unverified" flag on the citation. If neither a cited-classical nor a confidently-attributable true story is available, go straight to constructed-and-labeled — an honest invention beats a shaky attribution.
5. **T — Turn.** Point the narrative at the reader, second person, present tense, specific enough to be answerable from their last two weeks. "Have you ever…?" is filler. "Whose clumsy-but-sincere words did you correct this week — and for whose sake?" bites. Deliver AFTER the narrative, never before — the story must get past the guard first.
6. **E — Enact.** Frame as experiment with an observable result ("next time X, do Y once; watch what happens to Z"), never as regimen. If it needs a calendar or willpower, it's too big.
7. **R — Ripples.** Say *why* each effect follows. Second order: what changes around the reader in weeks. Third order: what compounds in the system — team, family, institution — over years. Include the inverse (what the absence selects for) when it sharpens.
8. **N — Nemesis.** Name how the ego performs this exact virtue — use the established term when one exists — and give the test that separates real from counterfeit. Pre-cleared terms with attributions: *spiritual bypassing* (John Welwood), *cheap grace* (Bonhoeffer, The Cost of Discipleship), *spiritual materialism* (Trungpa, Cutting Through Spiritual Materialism), *the Socratic pose* and *virtue signaling* (common usage). **Each counterfeit is a scene, not a summary** — build it with three parts: (a) a recognizable *moment* from the reader's world (a meeting, a decision, a sentence someone actually says), (b) an externally observable *tell* (what a bystander could watch for), and (c) the discriminating *test* (specific, dated, answerable — "name the last decision where…"). Include the positive sign too: what the real virtue looks and feels like when it's working. When the counterfeits share a root (they usually do — some form of status- or self-image-protection), name it in one closing line; the unifying diagnosis is the landing. A Nemesis that doesn't sting is a strawman; rewrite it.

**Multi-concept sets:** sequence as a curriculum (the Buddha's *anupubbikatha*, graduated talk) — prerequisites first (self-knowledge before self-transcendence), not importance-first.

## Output template

```
## LANTERN — <Concept name>

**Line.** <the aphorism> <(citation if borrowed)>

**Anchor.** <the daily-life image, one short paragraph>

**Narrative.** <the parable, told well, reversal intact> <(source or "constructed")>

**Turn.** <the mirror question>

**Enact.** <the one experiment>

**Ripples.** <second order …> <third order …>

**Nemesis.** <the counterfeits as scenes + the discriminating tests + the shared root>

---
*Sources: <citations, verification status, [INFERENCE] flags>*
```

The bold move-labels above are for saved/reference artifacts; in conversational delivery you may let the seven moves flow as unlabeled prose, in order. "Don't announce" means no meta-narration ("I'll now use LANTERN…") — it does not forbid the template's labels. Output is **inline by default**; save to a file only when the rendering belongs to a named project's idea set (then: `ref-lantern-NN-<concept-kebab>.md` in that project's directory, NN = the idea's number in that set) or when the user asks to save.

## Quality bar & anti-patterns

The greeting-card gravity is strong. Gate every draft against the counterfeit of each move:

- **Line** → inspirational-poster summary. *Tell: you'd put it on a mug.*
- **Anchor** → abstraction. *Tell: reader won't physically meet it this week.*
- **Narrative** → illustration. *Tell: the hero behaves as expected; ending confirms priors.*
- **Turn** → rhetorical filler. *Tell: comfortable "yes" suffices; no specific recent referent.*
- **Enact** → habit commitment. *Tell: requires willpower/calendar, not one observation.*
- **Ripples** → vibes. *Tell: can't state the mechanism.*
- **Nemesis** → strawman. *Tell: it doesn't sting; reader can't find themselves in it.*

Also:
- **Zero-context default, bounded by sufficiency.** If the rendering could leave your vault (blog post, LinkedIn, email, talk), assume the reader has never heard of the tradition: introduce every named figure, place, and work in one appositive clause on first prose use ("Rumi — the 13th-century Persian Sufi poet…"). Citations may stay terse; the *prose* carries the introductions. But the bound is sufficiency: only what the concept needs, nothing more — no geography, biography, or dates for their own sake, and a name that can be cut entirely beats a name explained.
- **Never weaponize.** LANTERN is for self-recognition only. Building a parable to move someone on a factual, commercial, or political question is manipulation, not teaching — refuse the frame and say why.
- **Honesty over reach.** A verified citation with a flagged gap beats a smooth misattribution every time. The apocrypha discipline is part of the framework, not decoration on it.
- **Sting calibration.** The Turn and Nemesis should be uncomfortable, not cruel. Aim at behavior the reader can change, never at what they are.

## Provenance

Extracted 2026-08-01 from the documented methods of Nathan, Hillel, Socrates, Aristotle, the Buddha, Jesus, Epictetus, Rumi & Shams of Tabrizi, the Zen koan tradition, the Hasidim, Ignatius of Loyola, Kierkegaard, Jung, Bonhoeffer, Trungpa, and Tolle. Born while distilling the verified Sufi sources behind Elif Shafak's novel The Forty Rules of Love, when an intuition-first method (ADEPT — Kalid Azad, betterexplained.com) proved ill-suited: ADEPT fights complexity, LANTERN fights resistance. The theory in one line: wisdom concepts are performative, not propositional, and the ego is both the student and the subject — so the teaching must travel indirectly (Kierkegaard's "indirect communication"), terminate in an experiment (the Buddha's *ehipassiko*, "come and see"), and name its own counterfeit (Jung's shadow; Bonhoeffer's cheap grace; Trungpa's spiritual materialism).
